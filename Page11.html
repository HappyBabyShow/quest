Porto Cathedral — Sé do Porto Perched atop the Pena Ventosa hill, this vantage point was once used to monitor the city, the Douro River, and approaching enemies. 1. Why Does the Cathedral Resemble a Fortress? Construction began in the 12th century, when Portugal was just emerging as an independent kingdom. Notice the two massive towers, thick granite walls, and narrow windows. Built in the Romanesque style, the cathedral strongly resembles a medieval castle. Above the main portal sits a large round rose window (rosácea), one of the cathedral’s best-preserved original medieval features. Over the centuries, later renovations added Gothic and Baroque architectural elements. 2. Royal Wedding — February 14, 1387 It was here that King John I of Portugal (Dom João I) married the English princess Philippa of Lancaster. This union was a critical diplomatic milestone that solidified the alliance between Portugal and England (established by the Treaty of Windsor). The city was adorned with flowers and aromatic herbs, and the royal wedding was celebrated with public festivities lasting for days. Curious detail: The ceremony took place on February 14, a date universally associated today with Valentine's Day. 3. Baptism of the Future Navigator In 1394, Prince Henry, son of John I and Philippa of Lancaster, was born in Porto. He was baptized in this cathedral and later became known to history as Henry the Navigator (Infante Dom Henrique). The prince was the driving force behind the organization and funding of Portugal's maritime expeditions down the western coast of Africa, though he personally took part in very few of the voyages associated with his name. 4. Interior Highlights * The Silver Altar: In the Chapel of the Holy Sacrament (Capela do Santíssimo Sacramento), you can admire an opulent silver altarpiece crafted by Porto's master silversmiths. * The Gothic Cloister: Tucked behind the sanctuary walls is a 14th-century Gothic cloister framed by vaulted arcades and decorated with magnificent 18th-century azulejo tile panels. 5. Serpents, Dragons, and Medieval Beasts Medieval artisans frequently carved mythical creatures, beasts, and expressive grotesque faces into religious monuments. In Christian iconography, dragons often personified evil, danger, and spiritual chaos, while their subjugation symbolized the triumph of virtue and divine order.
